Credit Default Risk Scoring for Consumer Credit Card Origination

AssignmentIndividual Assignment 1 (5%)

Summary. A Vietnamese consumer finance company approves credit cards with a manual scorecard and a large team of underwriters. This document designs an ML system that estimates each applicant's probability of missing the next payment and turns it into one of three underwriting actions: APPROVE, REVIEW or DECLINE. The design covers requirements, a hierarchy of goals and metrics, the architecture and pipeline, and five design trade-offs. The feasibility figures come from a prototype trained on a dataset with the UCI "Default of Credit Card Clients" schema (30,000 customers, 23 features, 23.3% default rate). This dataset stands in for the company's own records, which are hypothetical.

1. Problem Definition

1.1 Context and background

The organisation is a mid-sized consumer finance company in Vietnam. It issues unsecured credit cards to salaried customers, mostly first-time borrowers aged 22–40. It receives about 6,000 card applications per business day through a mobile app, a partner network of electronics retailers and branch offices. Unsecured consumer credit is a high-margin, high-loss business. The portfolio's 90-day delinquency rate is around 6–8%. Charge-offs are the largest cost line after funding, so a small improvement in who gets approved has a large effect on profit.

1.2 Problem statement

The current process cannot reliably tell customers who will repay apart from customers who will not. It is also too slow and too expensive to scale. In measurable terms:

  • Loss: about 22–23% of approved accounts miss at least one payment within the observation window. The current scorecard ranks risk with an estimated ROC AUC of about 0.68.
  • Cost and speed: about 35% of applications go to manual review, and the median time to a decision is 26 hours. Partners at the retail point of sale need an answer in under a minute, or the customer walks away.
  • Consistency: two underwriters looking at the same file agree only about 80% of the time. Decisions cannot be explained consistently to customers or to the regulator.

The goal is a service that returns a calibrated default probability and a decision in real time. It should reduce credit losses and manual workload without increasing unfair treatment between customer groups.

1.3 Current situation

Today a rule-based scorecard assigns points for age, income band, employment type and a credit bureau (CIC) flag. Any application that scores between two cut-offs, or that has an adverse CIC record, goes to an underwriter. The rules were last recalibrated two years ago. Nobody measures their performance systematically, and they use none of the behavioural data the company already holds for existing customers: repayment status, statement amounts and payments over the last six months.

1.4 Why machine learning

  • Risk depends on non-linear interactions, for example credit utilisation combined with a recent payment delay. Hand-written rules capture these poorly, but gradient-boosted trees and regularised logistic regression with engineered ratios learn them from data.
  • Labelled history exists, and every month of operation produces more of it. Each account eventually shows whether it defaulted, so the problem is standard supervised binary classification.
  • The decision is high-volume and repetitive. The value lies in consistency and speed, which automated scoring provides and manual review does not.
  • The environment changes, through marketing campaigns, the economic cycle and new partner channels. A model can be retrained and monitored for drift. A static scorecard silently decays.

2. Requirements Analysis

2.1 Functional requirements

IDRequirement
FR1Core ML function: estimate the probability that an applicant defaults on the next payment (binary classification, calibrated output in [0, 1]).
FR2Map the probability to an action using two thresholds held in configuration, not in code: < 0.30 → LOW/APPROVE; 0.30–0.60 → MEDIUM/REVIEW; ≥ 0.60 → HIGH/DECLINE.
FR3Provide a single-application endpoint (POST /predict) and a batch endpoint of up to 500 applications (POST /predict/batch) for nightly portfolio re-scoring and credit-limit reviews.
FR4Return the top factors behind each decision (POST /explain, SHAP), used for underwriter review and adverse-action notices.
FR5Expose /health and /model/info, the latter giving the model version, training date, metrics and the thresholds in force.
FR6Reject malformed input at the edge with HTTP 422, for example an unknown education code, age outside 18–100 or a negative payment.
FR7Log every request's features, score, decision and model version to support monitoring, audit and future labelling.

Input/output specification. The input is one JSON application with credit limit, demographics (sex, education, marital status, age) and three lists of six monthly values: repayment status (−2 to 8), statement amount and amount paid. The output is {default_probability, risk_band, decision, review_threshold, decline_threshold, model_version}. Both contracts are enforced by Pydantic schemas and published as OpenAPI.

Integration. The loan origination system (LOS) calls the scoring API synchronously over the internal network. Feature data for existing customers comes from the core banking system. Bureau data comes from the CIC gateway, and the LOS merges it before calling. Decisions and scores are written back to the LOS. Outcomes (default or no default) arrive from core banking 30–60 days later and are joined to the prediction log to create new training labels.

User interaction. Applicants see only the decision. Underwriters see the score, the risk band and the top explanatory factors in the review screen. Risk managers see monitoring dashboards and model reports. There is no direct user access to the model.

3. Goals and Metrics

The goals form a hierarchy. Each model metric is chosen because it moves a system goal, and each system goal supports a business goal. Model metrics can be computed offline today. Business metrics lag by 1–3 months, which is why leading indicators are monitored as well.

Baselines. (1) The prior baseline predicts the base rate for everyone: ROC AUC 0.50, PR AUC 0.233. This is the floor. (2) The legacy scorecard has an estimated ROC AUC of about 0.68, which is the operational baseline to beat. (3) In the prototype, a logistic regression on raw features reaches ROC AUC 0.745 and PR AUC 0.545, and gradient boosting reaches 0.747–0.751. The target of ≥ 0.75 is therefore realistic. The quality gate of ≥ 0.70 blocks a regression without causing false alarms on normal run-to-run variation.

Why these metrics. Accuracy is excluded because a model that approves everyone scores 77% accuracy. ROC AUC measures ranking and is independent of the threshold, so it is the primary metric. PR AUC and recall reflect the minority class, which is where the losses are. The Brier score matters because the thresholds are applied to raw probabilities. The fairness gap is a gate, not a report: a model that improves AUC but widens the gap past 0.10 is rejected.

4. High-level Architecture Design

4.1 Data flow

  1. Online. The LOS sends an application to POST /predict. The schema validates it. The fitted pipeline derives six features (utilisation, payment ratio, worst delay, months delayed, average bill, average payment), encodes and scales them, and scores. The decision rule maps the score to an action. The response goes back to the LOS, and the features, score and model version go to the prediction log and the in-memory drift window.
  2. Monitoring. Every 10 s Prometheus scrapes /metrics: request rate, errors and latency, the score histogram, decision counts, PSI per feature against the frozen training reference, and selection rate by group. Alert rules page on-call staff for service failures and notify the risk team about drift and fairness.
  3. Offline. Airflow runs the training DAG weekly, or on a drift alert: ingest a dated snapshot → validate → engineer features → train (every run tracked in MLflow) → evaluate, including per-group slices → quality gate → register. A model that passes the gate and beats the champion by ≥ 0.002 AUC becomes @champion; one that passes but does not beat it becomes @challenger; one that fails is registered and tagged rejected, which keeps an audit trail.
  4. Feedback. Outcomes are joined to the prediction log after 30–60 days. They become new training data and also produce the lagging accuracy metrics that confirm or refute what the drift signals suggested.

5. Trade-off Analysis

5.1 Accuracy vs latency (and explainability)

A large gradient-boosting ensemble, or a stack of models, might add 0.005–0.01 AUC. However, it costs latency and makes explanations harder. In the prototype, logistic regression with engineered features (ROC AUC 0.751) was at least as accurate as boosted trees (0.747–0.748) and scored in microseconds per row. Decision: serve a single Pipeline model that is either regularised logistic regression or a moderate HistGradientBoosting model (depth ≤ 8). The p95 budget is 100 ms. Explanations are computed on demand through a separate endpoint, so their cost (about 10–100 ms) never slows down /predict.

5.2 Freshness vs cost

Retraining continuously or daily would track the population closely. However, labels arrive 30–60 days late, so daily retraining would mostly refit on the same labels at the cost of compute, validation and review. Decision: retrain on a schedule (weekly to monthly) and also when drift is significant (PSI ≥ 0.25 sustained for 15 minutes). Drift is monitored in real time, but only a gated retrain changes the model. The drift reference stays frozen to the training data so that slow drift remains visible.

5.3 Simplicity vs performance

An online feature store, streaming features and a multi-model ensemble could extract more signal, but each adds infrastructure the team has to operate. Decision: use a monolithic sklearn Pipeline artifact with feature derivation inside it, one stateless API and batch retraining. This removes training–serving skew by construction and keeps rollback to a single alias change. More complexity is added only if the lagging business metric shows that the gap to the target cannot be closed otherwise.

5.4 Automation vs control

Fully automatic promotion could deploy a model that is more accurate but unfair, or one trained on a broken extract. Decision: automate the checks but keep humans on the thresholds. The gate (AUC ≥ 0.70, PR AUC ≥ 0.45, fairness gap ≤ 0.10, where a missing metric fails) and the champion margin decide promotion; rejected models are still registered for audit; production deployment needs approval by a risk officer through a protected GitHub environment; business thresholds are configuration owned by risk management. The model never auto-approves when it is down.

5.5 Privacy vs personalisation

Adding more personal data, such as phone metadata, social data or precise location, would probably improve AUC but raises consent, legal and fairness risks under Decree 13/2023/ND-CP. Decision: use only credit-relevant behavioural data that was collected with consent. Sensitive attributes (sex, age) are always kept for monitoring fairness. The prototype still uses them as inputs, because it follows the UCI schema. Removing sex before production, and retraining to measure the AUC cost against the change in the fairness gap, is a planned step reviewed with compliance, not something already done. Identifiers are hashed, and personal data never leaves the country. The model's AUC is accepted as the price of a defensible feature set.

5.6 Known risks

  • Selective labels: declined applicants never show outcomes, so the training data comes only from past approvals. Mitigation: a small randomised review band, and reject-inference analysis.
  • Feedback loops and drift: marketing campaigns change the applicant population (for example younger customers with lower limits). This is mitigated by PSI per feature, score quantiles and decision-mix alerts.
  • Accuracy vs fairness: the most accurate family (logistic regression) had the widest selection-rate gap (0.054 against 0.028–0.037 for tree models). The gate makes this trade-off explicit rather than accidental.
